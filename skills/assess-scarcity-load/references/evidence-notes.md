## The framing

**Mullainathan, S., & Shafir, E. (2013). *Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much*. Henry Holt / Picador.** Verified by fetch (OpenLibrary record, https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780805092646.json).
The book-length statement of the scarcity account: having too little of something (money, time) captures attention, which helps with the shortfall itself and hurts everything else, including planning, remembering, and resisting impulses. The "bandwidth" vocabulary comes from here. Use it for the mechanism; it reports no single effect size this skill should quote.

**Haushofer, J., & Fehr, E. (2014). On the psychology of poverty. *Science*. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1232491** Verified by Crossref (content not fetched in the audit).
A review of how poverty affects decision making, including stress and attention. Cite it as the broader review; do not attribute specific findings to it, since the audit did not read it.

**ideas42, Daminger, Hayes, Barrows & Wright (2015). *Poverty Interrupted*.** [unverified, author to confirm].
Practitioner report with the line "poverty is a tax... in time, money and cognition" (published 2015, not 2016). Not re-fetched in the audit; use the phrase only with the tag.

## The evidence

**Mani, A., Mullainathan, S., Shafir, E., & Zhao, J. (2013). Poverty impedes cognitive function. *Science*, 341, 976. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1238041** Verified by Crossref (abstract not fetched; effect size unverified).
Two studies: a lab study with shoppers in the United States, and a field study with sugarcane farmers in India tested before and after harvest. The field design is the one this skill borrows (the same people at a tight and a slack moment). The dossier records no effect size, so this skill does not quote one, and the lab half is covered by the contested status below.

**Kaur, S., Mullainathan, S., Oh, S., & Schilbach, F. (2025). Do financial concerns make workers less productive? *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 140(1), 635–689. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae038** Verified by Crossref (published-version details from the NBER page for w28338, https://doi.org/10.3386/w28338, also verified; audit year 2024, online-first).
Field experiment with low-income piece-rate manufacturing workers in India. Per the NBER abstract: paying workers earlier raised output by 7% (0.11 SD) and reduced costly, unintentional mistakes; the effects were concentrated among the most financially constrained. This is the strongest evidence in the entry and the basis for the recommendation "time cash to arrive when constraints bind". It is one experiment in one setting; the dossier gives no meta-analysis.

**O'Donnell, M., et al. (2021). Empirical audit and review and an assessment of evidentiary value in research on the psychological consequences of scarcity. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2103313118** Verified by Crossref.
An audit of 20 scarcity-priming studies (the lab paradigm in which participants are reminded of financial strain before a cognitive task). Per the dossier: mixed results. This is why the lab side of the literature is called contested and why the skill does not lean on priming effects.

**Bryan, C. J., Tipton, E., & Yeager, D. S. (2021). Behavioural science is unlikely to change the world without a heterogeneity revolution. *Nature Human Behaviour*, 5(8), 980–989. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01143-3** Verified by Crossref.
Argues that behavioral effects should be expected to vary across people and settings, and that studies should measure moderators rather than rely on convenience samples. Cited here because Kaur's effect was concentrated among the most constrained, so this skill rates load per subgroup. The fetched text did not discuss WEIRD samples; do not attribute a WEIRD claim to it.

## Non-WEIRD evidence

Both field studies are from India: sugarcane farmers (Mani et al. 2013) and low-income piece-rate manufacturing workers (Kaur et al. 2025). The contested priming studies are mostly from United States and online samples. The India evidence is the strong part of this entry, which is why the skill is tagged mixed-evidence rather than weird-only: the mechanism has field support outside WEIRD samples, but it has been tested in two livelihoods, and the calendar that matters is local.

## Replication picture, stated plainly

- Lab scarcity priming: contested. A published comment on the 2013 *Science* paper exists (the dossier mentions it without a citation; do not cite it from memory) and the O'Donnell et al. audit of 20 studies gave mixed results.
- Field timing of cash: strong on the strength of one large experiment (Kaur et al. 2025). No pooled estimate exists in the dossier.
- The practical consequence: recommendations about timing cash or the ask relative to the income calendar rest on better evidence than recommendations that rely on "people under scarcity think worse" as a general claim.

## Known corrections and cautions from the dossier

- Do not quote a Mani et al. effect size.
- The report's "replication critiques exist" is covered by O'Donnell et al.; do not cite other critiques from memory.
- Kaur et al. is published as *QJE* 140(1), 635–689 (2025), DOI 10.1093/qje/qjae038; the NBER working paper is w28338.
- The load rubric (high, medium, low) in SKILL.md is the drafter's judgment aid, not a scale from any of these sources, and SKILL.md says so.

## What to check locally

- The actual income calendar for each subgroup the program serves: inflow months, reliability, and the outflows participants themselves name as tight (school fees, festivals, planting inputs, loan repayments, rent).
- Whether the "tight period" is the same for everyone in the program or differs by livelihood; rate by subgroup if it differs.
- Whether the program can change the timing of the ask at all, or only the design; and whether it controls any payment it could re-time.
- Whether the household has the money for the behavior at any point in the cycle; if not, the finding is money, and this skill's recommendations do not apply.
- Whether a reminder or a simplification removes a safeguard (consent, a cooling-off period) for financial or health asks.
- Whether the audience and context brief's calendar was observed or assumed; an assumed row is the first thing to confirm with five to ten participants.
