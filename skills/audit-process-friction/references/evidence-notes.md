## The framing

**Sunstein, C. R. (2020). Sludge audits. *Behavioural Public Policy*. https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2019.32** Verified by Crossref.
Introduces the sludge audit: an institution counts the friction (paperwork, waiting, reporting, decisions) it imposes on people seeking something they are entitled to, and justifies each piece. Conceptual and policy paper; no effect size.

**Sunstein, C. R. (2021). *Sludge: What Stops Us from Getting Things Done and What to Do about It*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/13859.001.0001** Verified by Crossref.
Book-length treatment of the same idea, with United States administrative examples. Use for the definition and the argument that sludge falls hardest on the people with the least time, money, and attention.

**Behavioural Insights Team (2014; revised and updated edition, c. 2024). *EAST: Four simple ways to apply behavioural insights*. London: BIT. https://bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/BIT-EAST.pdf** Verified by fetch (the PDF at this URL is the revised edition; the preface dates the original to 2014; no individual authors on the cover).
"Make it easy" is the first of the four rules. The revised edition flags studies that "have not held up to scrutiny", so cite EAST for the principle, not for any specific study inside it.

**Gyani, A., Tindall, K., & Gallagher, R. (2022). *Explore Field Guide: A practical tool to map and unpack behaviour*. BIT. https://www.bi.team/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/BIT-Explore-Field-Guide.pdf** Verified by fetch.
The administrative-data analysis section (four steps) is the nearest published procedure for turning counts per step into a loss estimate; cite it for the procedure of step 5.

**Datta, S., & Mullainathan, S. (2014). Behavioral design: A new approach to development policy. *Review of Income and Wealth*, 60(1), 7–35. https://doi.org/10.1111/roiw.12093** Verified by Crossref (earlier version: CGD Policy Paper 016, 2012, verified by fetch).
Supplies the Define, Diagnose, Design, Test cycle and the reading of the Kenya fertilizer result as a "small hassle cost of travel". It is their interpretation, not a separate experiment.

## The evidence

**Bettinger, E. P., Long, B. T., Oreopoulos, P., & Sanbonmatsu, L. (2012). The role of application assistance and information in college decisions: Results from the H&R Block FAFSA experiment. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 127(3), 1205–1242. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs017** Verified by Crossref.
Randomized experiment in United States tax-preparation offices. Per the dossier: assistance in completing the federal student-aid form raised two-year college completion from 28% to 36%; an information-only arm had no effect. This is the anchor for ranking assistance above information. Sample size and subgroup details are not recorded in the dossier; do not add them from memory.

**Duflo, E., Kremer, M., & Robinson, J. (2011). Nudging farmers to use fertilizer: Theory and experimental evidence from Kenya. *American Economic Review*, 101(6), 2350–2390. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.6.2350** Verified by Crossref (NBER w15131, https://doi.org/10.3386/w15131, also verified).
Western Kenya, 1,230 maize farmers. A small, time-limited free-delivery offer made just after harvest raised fertilizer use by 14 points on a base of 24% in season 1 and 18 points on a base of 26% in season 2; later discounts worked less well (numbers from the dossier's `choose-delivery-moment` entry). The offer combined timing with delivery, so it is evidence about a small hassle plus a well-timed moment, not about hassle alone.

**Banerjee, A. V., Duflo, E., Glennerster, R., & Kothari, D. (2010). Improving immunisation coverage in rural India: Clustered randomised controlled evaluation of immunisation campaigns with and without incentives. *BMJ*, 340, c2220. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.c2220** Verified by Crossref.
Rural Rajasthan, clustered randomized evaluation. Per the dossier: reliable monthly immunization camps on their own (supply-side reliability, no incentive) raised full immunization from 6% to 18%. The hassle removed was the wasted trip to a camp that might not be running.

**DellaVigna, S., & Linos, E. (2022). RCTs to scale: Comprehensive evidence from two nudge units. *Econometrica*, 90(1), 81–116. https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709** Verified by Crossref.
126 RCTs, 23 million people. Academic journal studies average 8.7 percentage points (+33.4%); nudge-unit trials average 1.4 points (+8.0%). About 70% of the gap is explained by selective publication combined with low power. This is the discount the skill applies to any expected-effect statement.

## Non-WEIRD evidence

Kenya (Duflo, Kremer & Robinson 2011) and India (Banerjee et al. 2010), both above. Both are field experiments that removed one specific hassle; neither ran a sludge audit as a method, and no dedicated LMIC sludge-audit trial was found in the dossier. The method's transfer claim therefore rests on the general principle plus single experiments, which is why the skill is tagged mixed-evidence.

## Known corrections and cautions from the dossier

- The dossier lists no corrections for this entry beyond the unverified items above.
- Do not generalize the Kenya result to "removing a trip raises take-up by 14 to 18 points"; the offer was also timed to the post-harvest moment, which `choose-delivery-moment` and `assess-scarcity-load` handle.
- Quote the FAFSA numbers as the dossier writes them (two-year college completion, 28% to 36%); the audit did not re-fetch the paper to confirm which outcome the percentages describe.

## What to check locally

- Which steps are required by law, a regulator, or a partner's rules, and who can confirm that; a step the program cannot remove is movable at best.
- Whether the walk-through was done in the user's conditions: their phone, their documents, their working hours, their language, their transport.
- The cost of a trip in this setting (transport, lost earnings, childcare), which the official flow never records.
- Whether any counts exist per step; if not, whether a two-week tally at the touchpoints is cheaper than guessing.
- Whether the people lost at each step differ from those who finish (the poorest, those without ID); sludge screens selectively, and the audit should say who is screened out.
- Whether a step that looks like sludge is doing protective work (consent, fraud, eligibility) before it is called removable.
