## Feedback intervention theory

### Kluger & DeNisi (1996) [verified-crossref]
Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. *Psychological Bulletin*, 119, 254–284. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254
- Meta-analysis: d = 0.41 across 607 effect sizes.
- More than one-third of feedback interventions reduced performance.
- Mechanism (feedback intervention theory): feedback directs attention to a gap between performance and a standard; feedback that turns attention to the self reduces performance; task-focused feedback helps.
- Population: the pre-1996 organizational and educational psychology literature; the dossier records no country breakdown, so treat it as WEIRD-skewed.

## Repeated comparison reports in the field

### Allcott (2011) [verified-crossref]
Allcott, H. (2011). Social norms and energy conservation. *Journal of Public Economics*, 95(9–10), 1082–1095. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2011.03.003
- Opower home energy reports, US households. Energy use fell 2.0%; the top decile cut 6.3%, the bottom decile 0.3%.
- The dossier gives the decile figures without defining the deciles; the skill reads them as deciles of usage (heaviest users moved most, lightest barely). Author to confirm the reading.

### Allcott & Rogers (2014) [verified-crossref]
Allcott, H., & Rogers, T. (2014). The short-run and long-run effects of behavioral interventions: Experimental evidence from energy conservation. *American Economic Review*, 104(10), 3003–3037. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.104.10.3003
- "Action and backsliding": recipients act after each report and backslide between reports.
- When reports stop after two years, effects decay 10–20% per year.

### Schultz, Nolan, Cialdini, Goldstein & Griskevicius (2007) [verified-crossref]
*Psychological Science*, 18(5), 429–434. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x
- Boomerang among low users (those already better than the norm) shown a descriptive peer comparison without an injunctive cue; the injunctive cue removes it.

## Cross-cutting cautions (dossier section A)

### DellaVigna & Linos (2022) [verified-crossref]
*Econometrica*, 90(1), 81–116. https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709
- 126 RCTs, 23 million people. Academic-journal nudges average 8.7 percentage points; nudge-unit trials 1.4 points. About 70% of the gap is selective publication combined with low power.

### Ferraro & Tracy (2022) [verified-crossref]
*Experimental Economics*, 25(5), 1441–1466. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-022-09754-x
- Loss-framed contracts: pooled 0.16 SD; lab 0.33 SD versus field 0.02 SD; publication bias among lab studies. The dossier's lab-to-field shrinkage example.

### Pattern the dossier asks every technique skill to state
Lab-to-field shrinkage (0.33 to 0.02 SD) and the academic-versus-unit gap (8.7 vs 1.4 points) point the same way: discount published lab or academic effects when transferring them to a field program.

## What each comparison type rests on
- **Own past:** no boomerang risk, because there is no norm to drift toward; weakest social pull. A design choice, not a separately tested comparison in the dossier.
- **Peers:** the Opower and Schultz evidence; strongest documented pull; boomerang among those above the norm unless an injunctive cue is added.
- **Standard:** the gap-to-standard mechanism in feedback intervention theory; no field benchmark in the dossier.

## What to check locally
1. Can each person's number be computed reliably every period, and will the person believe it?
2. Is a peer comparison private here, or do people know who "the average" is?
3. Is there an injunctive cue the population reads as approval (a word, a mark, a symbol) rather than as sarcasm?
4. Will supervisors see the report, and what will they do with it?
5. Who is already above the norm, and what happened to them in the pilot?
