# Worked example: informal private providers in rural Rajasthan, India

An illustrative run. The program (call it "Sahi Ilaj", illustrative) trains informal private providers in two blocks of Udaipur district, Rajasthan, on managing acute watery diarrhea in adults and on referring presumptive tuberculosis to the government testing center. Its measurement plan names provider practice as the primary outcome, measured before and six months after training. All numbers below are illustrative, not findings.

**Answers to Before you start:** (1) informal private providers (no formal medical degree) in two blocks; the behavior is the management of two scripted adult cases; clients walk in without appointment. (2) Both cases can be presented by a healthy adult with words alone; purchases are kept, nothing is taken. (3) The organization has an ethics board and a local institutional review board; the providers are private, not public employees, and the district health office has agreed in writing to aggregate reporting only. (4) A list of 120 providers from a program census; one visit per provider per case; repeated at six months. (5) The national guideline for the two conditions, to be confirmed by the program's clinical adviser.

# Mystery client protocol: management of adult diarrhea and presumptive TB by informal private providers

**Provider behavior measured:** informal private providers' history taking, treatment, and referral for two scripted adult cases **Setting:** private informal clinics and medicine shops, two rural blocks, Udaipur district
**Purpose:** evaluation outcome, before and six months after training **Providers are public employees:** no; district health office agreed in writing to aggregate reporting only

## Cases
### Case 1: adult acute watery diarrhea
- Opening statement: "Since the day before yesterday I have had loose motions, five or six times a day. What should I take?"
- Answers to likely questions: watery, no blood; no fever; drinking water from the village hand pump; no vomiting; no one else at home ill; ate at a wedding three days ago; no prior treatment; can pay up to 150 rupees (illustrative)
- Refusal and safety rules: no injection ("I am afraid of needles, give me something to take at home"); buy what is prescribed, keep the packet and the receipt or note the price; do not drink anything given on site; if the provider insists on an injection or a drip, say a relative is waiting and leave
- Correct management per the national guideline (for clinical review): ask about blood, fever, duration, fluid intake, and signs of dehydration; advise oral rehydration solution and continued fluids; no antibiotic for watery diarrhea without blood or fever; advise when to return
### Case 2: adult cough of three weeks
- Opening statement: "I have had a cough for about three weeks now, and some fever in the evenings. The cough syrup from the shop did not help."
- Answers to likely questions: dry cough turning productive; evening fever; some weight loss and tiredness; no blood in sputum; no one at home with TB that they know of; no prior TB test; works as a day laborer; can pay up to 200 rupees (illustrative)
- Refusal and safety rules: no injection; buy what is prescribed and keep it; accept a referral slip; if an X-ray is proposed on site, say it will be done in town and leave
- Correct management per the national guideline (for clinical review): ask about duration, fever, weight loss, sputum, blood, and household contacts; refer for sputum testing at the government center; no steroid; advise on infection precautions

## Essential-action checklist
| Action | Essential / recorded only | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Asked about blood in stool (case 1); about duration and sputum (case 2) | essential | national guideline, history section |
| Asked about fever; about weight loss or contacts (case 2) | essential | same |
| Advised ORS and fluids (case 1); referred for sputum test (case 2) | essential | treatment and referral sections |
| Prescribed or sold an antibiotic for case 1; a steroid for case 2 | recorded only (harmful action) | guideline |
| Offered or gave an injection | recorded only (harmful action) | |
| Price charged; consultation time; items purchased | recorded only | |

## Standardized clients
Eight local adults (four men, four women, aged 25 to 45), recruited from a neighboring block so the providers do not know them; five days of training with mock consultations until two trainers score the same consultation identically on every essential line; recall to the checklist on a phone form within 15 minutes of leaving, out of sight of the clinic.

## Ethics plan (for the organization's ethics board)
- Justification for deception: announced observation changes what informal providers do and say; a scripted unannounced visit measures ordinary practice with no real patient at risk
- Consent: provider-level advance consent at the program census, with an opt-out, worded "in the coming months a trained person may visit your clinic as a client to study how care is given in this area; you may decline now"; providers who decline are excluded
- No harm to the client: no injection, nothing taken, purchases kept; no harm to providers: aggregate results by block and provider type only; no individual results to any authority
- Dangerous-practice rule: if a provider proposes an injection or drip for the client, the client declines and leaves; the visit is recorded; no report is made to authorities, per the consent terms
- Data handling: provider identifiers replaced by codes at entry; the code key held by the program lead only

## Sampling of providers and visits
Frame: 120 consenting providers from the census; one visit per provider per case (240 visits per round); ordinary morning and evening hours; each client visits a provider once in each round; same design at baseline and at six months, with clients rotated across cases.

## Detection and debrief
Clients record suspected detection and cues after every visit; at six months, a provider survey asks whether they suspected a standardized client and when; visits flagged as detected by either source are analyzed separately and excluded from the main estimate; all consenting providers receive the aggregate results at a block meeting after the second round.

## Analysis
Adherence = share of essential actions completed per visit; correct-management rate per case; harmful-action rate (antibiotic, steroid, injection); price and time; comparison baseline to six months and medicine shops versus clinics; detected visits reported separately; the unit is the visit, standard errors clustered by provider.

## What to check before relying on this
- Case 2 (three-week cough) is the one most likely to draw an X-ray or injection proposal; the exit line must be rehearsed and the clinical adviser must confirm it is safe to present
- The antibiotic line for case 1 depends on the guideline's wording for watery diarrhea without blood or fever; confirm with the clinical review before training
